The volute bore

A metre of 10 × 10mm bore wound into a 192mm disc, drawn as six semicircles of stepping radius. Every arc is constant‑radius, which is the whole reason it works.

Drawn to scale at 1 unit = 1mm. The band is the plywood cheek, 20mm wide; the pale channel inside it is the 10mm airway.

Why arcs, and not a spiral

A true spiral’s radius is still changing across each facet, and the offsetting code mitres a vertex assuming the curvature either side of it is constant. That is true of an arc and false of a spiral, and it cost 6.44mm of a 10mm airway — the same class of fault as a bore that measures 10 by 7.

Here the radius holds across each arc and steps only at the vertices, where a mitre already expects a corner. It is the construction every other shape in the repository already uses.

The centre end has no way out

A volute winds inward and ends at the centre, and that end is enclosed. Measured against the rest of the bore, the inner lead comes within 7.75mm of its neighbours where it needs 20mm, and a straight lead leaving the centre runs 1mm before it is inside the coil. There is no direction it can leave in.

So this is a correct bore path and not yet an instrument. Both openings have to reach the rim, which means the true double spiral: wind in, then back out between the turns already there.

What is solved

The construction. Constant‑radius arcs keep the airway exact, the six radii step cleanly from 82.3 to 22.3mm, it lands on 1000.7mm, and the tightest arc clears the tooth floor at R22.3 against R19.6.

What is not

Interleaving the return. Every version I built retraced exactly over the inward path — 0.00mm clearance. The outward passes need to sit half a step off the inward ones, and the volute’s shifting centres do not give me that for free the way a plain spiral would.
